\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:litreview}

This chapter surveys the prior work that underpins the five technical
pillars of the thesis: distributed training, high-performance GEMM
libraries, efficient attention mechanisms, LLM serving runtimes, and
GPU-kernel profiling. The aim is to establish where the state of the art
currently stands, to highlight the open gaps the thesis attempts to
close, and to introduce the vocabulary used throughout the remaining
chapters. The chapter follows the same top-down order as the stack
itself (Fig.~\ref{fig:intro_stack}): we start at the outermost level
of the training schedule and progressively descend to kernel-level
profiling.

\paragraph{How to read this chapter.} Sections~\ref{sec:lit_dist}
through~\ref{sec:lit_profiling} each follow the same internal
structure: a brief restatement of the classical problem, a survey of
the principal systems or papers that address it, and a pointer
forward to the chapter of the thesis that reopens the question. The
final section (Section~\ref{sec:lit_gap}) collects the open gaps
into a single positioning statement that is referred back to in the
contributions list of Section~\ref{sec:intro_contributions}. Readers
familiar with a given sub-area can skip directly to the forward
pointer in its section.

\section{Distributed Training of Deep Neural Networks}\label{sec:lit_dist}

\subsection{Data Parallelism and Its Refinements}\label{sec:lit_dp}
Data parallelism (DP) is the oldest and conceptually simplest distributed
training strategy: every worker holds a replica of the model, processes a
disjoint shard of the mini-batch, and participates in an all-reduce that
synchronises gradients across replicas. Its main limitation is memory
duplication and the communication cost of synchronising a full gradient
tensor on every step. Ott~et~al.~\cite{ott2018fairseq} showed that
large-batch training with well-tuned learning-rate schedules recovers
single-device accuracy while spreading the work across many GPUs, and
You~et~al.~\cite{you2020lars} introduced the LARS/LAMB optimisers that
stabilise very large batches.

The PyTorch Distributed Data Parallel path~\cite{li2020pytorch}
is the engineering answer to this. It groups gradients into
communication \emph{buckets} whose size matches the granularity of
the backward-pass computation, so that each bucket finishes its
reduction while the next layer is still computing; it runs those
reductions on a dedicated CUDA stream that is hooked into the
autograd engine; and it dispatches them through a ring topology
whose bandwidth consumption per worker is asymptotically independent
of the world size. The classical $2(P-1)/P$ bandwidth factor of a
ring all-reduce is a consequence of the two-phase
reduce-scatter/all-gather structure, and the latency term that scales
with the number of hops becomes negligible once the parameter tensor
is larger than a few tens of megabytes, which is always the case
for the models in this thesis. DDP is therefore the industry
baseline for single-node multi-GPU training and the reference point
against which FSDP and ZeRO must justify their extra collectives.

\subsection{Model Parallelism and Pipeline Parallelism}\label{sec:lit_mp}
As models outgrow a single accelerator, model parallelism becomes
necessary. Shoeybi~et~al.~\cite{shoeybi2019megatron} introduced
Megatron-LM, a tensor-parallel framework that shards attention and MLP
layers across devices using dimension-wise splits, preserving a single
training step as a sequence of all-reduces. Narayanan et
al.~\cite{narayanan2021efficient} extended this to trillion-parameter
scale by combining tensor parallelism with pipeline parallelism on a
$3072$-GPU cluster, achieving $52\%$ of peak.

Pipeline parallelism splits the model into sequential stages executed on
different devices; its main challenge is the \emph{pipeline bubble}
produced by warm-up and cool-down phases. GPipe~\cite{huang2019gpipe}
reduces the bubble by processing multiple micro-batches concurrently and
pays a memory overhead proportional to the number of micro-batches in
flight. PipeDream~\cite{harlap2018pipedream} interleaves forward and
backward passes (the $1\mathrm{F}1\mathrm{B}$ schedule) and recovers
near-linear scaling at the price of mild gradient staleness.

\subsection{Sharded Data Parallelism: FSDP and ZeRO}\label{sec:lit_shard}
Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP)~\cite{zhao2023pytorch} shards
parameters, gradients, and optimiser state across the data-parallel
group. Parameters are all-gathered just before a layer's forward or
backward pass and re-sharded immediately afterwards, keeping peak
per-GPU memory independent of the model size. The engineering effort
in FSDP is in the \emph{unit of sharding}: too coarse and the all-gather
traffic becomes bursty, too fine and the launch overhead dominates.
PyTorch FSDP-2 introduces the \texttt{FlatParameter} abstraction that
collapses each transformer block into a single sharded tensor, tuning
the unit of communication to the unit of compute.

The DeepSpeed ZeRO family~\cite{rajbhandari2019zero, rajbhandari2021zero}
groups the sharding question into three levels that each reclaim
memory from a different part of the training state. The first level
keeps one copy of the parameters on every device but shards the
Adam moments, which is where most of the memory sits once the
master FP32 copy is accounted for. The second level additionally
shards the gradients that feed into those moments, so that a worker
never materialises the full gradient tensor except during the
reduce-scatter that produces its own shard. The third level
shards the parameters themselves: each worker is responsible for a
slice of the model and must gather the other slices on demand
before every forward and backward pass. The third level reaches the
information-theoretic lower bound on per-worker memory, and in
exchange it pays two extra collectives per layer---an all-gather
before compute and a reduce-scatter afterwards---both of which must
be overlapped with compute to avoid a regression on step time.
ZeRO-Infinity~\cite{rajbhandari2021zero} adds NVMe offloading, enabling
trillion-parameter training on $512$-GPU systems. DeepSpeed itself
\cite{rasley2020deepspeed, rajbhandari2022deepspeed} packages ZeRO with
gradient compression, loss scaling, and Mixture-of-Experts support, and
is widely used in production LLM training pipelines.

Korthikanti~et~al.~\cite{korthikanti2022activation} showed that for
models beyond $\sim 10$B parameters, the \emph{activation} memory
becomes the binding constraint rather than the parameter memory, and
introduced selective activation recomputation---an idea originally
developed by Chen~et~al.~\cite{chen2016training} for smaller models.
Most public comparisons of DP/DDP/FSDP/ZeRO report individual results
in isolation rather than a controlled, single-node study;
Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed} addresses that gap with a common model,
dataset, and hardware configuration.

\subsection{Mixed Precision and Optimiser
Considerations}\label{sec:lit_mp_amp}
Micikevicius~et~al.~\cite{micikevicius2018amp} showed that training in
FP16 with a master FP32 copy of the weights and dynamic loss scaling
preserves accuracy while halving memory usage and doubling Tensor-Core
throughput. BF16 on A100 and FP8 on H100~\cite{nvidia_hopper} extend this
further at the cost of a small numerical gap that has to be managed at
the layer level (usually through careful placement of the accumulator
precision).

\subsection{Collective Communication Libraries}\label{sec:lit_nccl}
Distributed training performance hinges on the collective library
underneath. NCCL~\cite{nccl} implements ring and tree all-reduce on top
of NVLink/NVSwitch/InfiniBand, with message-size-aware algorithm
selection. Castell{\'o}~et~al.~\cite{castello2021accelerating} describe
pipelined all-reduce on top of MPI, showing that overlapping reduce and
broadcast stages with intra-node copies yields up to $30\%$ end-to-end
speedup on commodity clusters.

\section{High-Performance GPU Kernels and GEMM Libraries}\label{sec:lit_gemm}

Matrix multiplication is the performance-critical primitive of every
transformer layer. Vendor libraries such as cuBLAS and cuBLASLt expose
highly tuned dense GEMM kernels but offer limited support for the
non-standard shapes, fused epilogues, and grouped variants that modern
LLMs require. CUTLASS~\cite{cutlass} provides a C++ template library of
GEMM building blocks with explicit control over tile sizes, software
pipelining, and Tensor-Core instructions. Its CuTe sublibrary generalises
layout algebra so that a single template can be instantiated for many
hardware generations and data types.

\subsection{From cuBLAS to CUTLASS}\label{sec:lit_cublas_cutlass}
cuBLAS is a closed-source library optimised by NVIDIA for a small number
of canonical GEMM shapes and precisions. For the skinny, batched, or
grouped GEMMs that dominate LLM attention and MoE layers, cuBLAS
frequently dispatches to a generic kernel that leaves Tensor Cores
under-utilised. CUTLASS exposes the three-level tile hierarchy
(threadblock, warp, instruction) explicitly so that a user can match
the kernel to their actual shape; it also composes with epilogue
templates that fuse bias, activation, and residual-add operations into
the writeback of the main GEMM.

\subsection{Triton and the DSL
Approach}\label{sec:lit_triton_dsl}
Triton~\cite{tillet2019triton} takes a different approach: it is a
Python-embedded DSL that exposes the tile abstraction directly, letting
kernel authors trade a modest fraction of peak throughput for much
faster iteration cycles. Recent work in
FlashAttention-2~\cite{dao2023flashattention2} and
FlashAttention-3~\cite{dao2024flashattention3} builds directly on these
primitives, and much of the serving stack discussed later in this
thesis is written in Triton. The literature contains relatively few
treatments that compare the two ecosystems on the exact kernels
produced by a transformer forward pass; this is one of the gaps that
Chapters~\ref{ch:cutlass} and~\ref{ch:triton} aim to narrow.

\subsection{Hardware-Specific Primitives}\label{sec:lit_hw}
The A100 whitepaper~\cite{nvidia_a100} documents the third-generation
Tensor Cores with $312$\,TFLOPS of FP16, the new \texttt{mma.m16n8k16}
and \texttt{mma.m16n8k8} instructions, and the \texttt{cp.async}
asynchronous copy path. The H100 whitepaper~\cite{nvidia_hopper}
introduces the Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA), fourth-generation Tensor
Cores, a dedicated transformer engine with FP8 support, and DSMEM for
threadblock-cluster cooperation. CUTLASS 3.x abstracts these differences
behind a layout-algebra interface so that the same kernel template can
target multiple architectures with different \texttt{Sm}-specific
instantiations.

\section{Efficient Attention Mechanisms}\label{sec:lit_attn}

\subsection{Exact Attention Acceleration}\label{sec:lit_flashattn}
The central insight of the Flash Attention family is that the full
$N \times N$ score matrix is an intermediate that need not reside in
HBM at any point: if queries are streamed against keys and values in
matching tiles, and the running row-max and row-sum are carried across
tiles via a correction factor, the output can be produced without ever
writing the dense scores back to global memory. The original
kernel~\cite{dao2022flashattention} uses this rearrangement to reduce
the memory footprint from $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ to $\mathcal{O}(N)$ while
remaining bit-equivalent to the dense reference up to the order of
floating-point additions. FlashAttention-2~\cite{dao2023flashattention2}
re-balances the work along the sequence axis so that the occupancy of
an A100 does not drop at long sequence lengths, and
FlashAttention-3~\cite{dao2024flashattention3} rewrites the same idea
against the Hopper WGMMA and FP8 pipelines, taking advantage of
asynchrony between the copy engine and the Tensor-Core pipeline. In
the space covered by this thesis, these three kernels are treated as
the reference point that any new attention kernel must match on
correctness and must beat on either speed or sparsity.

\subsection{Sparse and Local Attention}\label{sec:lit_sparse}
A second line of work attacks the quadratic cost at the definitional
level, by dropping the dependence between distant tokens. The Sparse
Transformer~\cite{child2019generating} was the first widely-adopted
example: it composes a strided pattern with a fixed pattern to reach
sequence lengths in the tens of thousands while keeping the per-layer
cost linear in $N$. Longformer~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} simplifies
this to a local sliding window augmented with a handful of global
tokens for task-specific anchoring, and BigBird~\cite{zaheer2020bigbird}
restores the universal-approximator property by layering a random
long-range term on top of the window. The current open-weight
generation of models has converged on the simplest of these patterns:
a plain sliding window of a few thousand positions, on the empirical
observation that the softmax weight outside that radius is already
indistinguishable from numerical noise. This architectural
convergence is what makes a window-aware kernel commercially
relevant, not merely academically interesting.

Linearised attention
(Performer~\cite{choromanski2020performer},
Linformer~\cite{wang2020linformer}) and
low-rank approximations
(Reformer~\cite{kitaev2020reformer}) take the complementary approach of
replacing the full attention by a cheaper-to-compute surrogate; they
trade asymptotic complexity for a small accuracy gap and have seen
modest adoption in production. The exact sliding-window path pursued in
Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} keeps the attention numerics unchanged and
exploits the sparsity at the kernel level rather than in the
mathematical definition.

\subsection{Kernel-Level Fusions for Attention}\label{sec:lit_fused}
Several kernel-level variants of attention target inference
specifically. PagedAttention~\cite{vllm} stores the key--value cache in
fixed-size pages so that the attention kernel can address it with
32-bit page indices, eliminating fragmentation.
RadixAttention~\cite{sglang} extends this to a radix tree so that shared
prompt prefixes are stored only once. Multi-Query
Attention (MQA)~\cite{shazeer2019mqa} and Grouped-Query Attention
(GQA)~\cite{ainslie2023gqa} reduce the KV cache by a factor of $H$ (the
number of heads) or $H/G$ (the number of groups), which directly
reduces the memory bandwidth required during decode. These designs
change the memory layout seen by the attention kernel and therefore
have direct consequences for kernel scheduling, discussed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}.

\subsection{Positional Encodings for Long
Context}\label{sec:lit_rope}
Long-context attention cannot be evaluated in isolation from the
positional encoding: rotary position embeddings
(RoPE)~\cite{su2021rope} extend cleanly to sequences longer than those
seen in training, and combined with dynamic NTK-aware scaling they
allow a model trained at $N = 4{,}096$ to be served at $N = 32{,}768$
with graceful degradation. SWFA composes with RoPE without
modification, because the window constraint acts purely on the
attention score matrix.

\subsection{Block Sparsity as a Design
Pattern}\label{sec:lit_block_sparsity}
A recurring theme across the above families is that \emph{block-level}
sparsity is practical while \emph{element-level} sparsity is not. The
reason is structural: modern Tensor Cores issue the same
$16 \times 8 \times 16$ MMA instruction regardless of how many
individual elements are zero, so element-level sparsity delivers no
wall-clock saving unless the hardware itself supports a structured
pruning format. Block sparsity, in contrast, maps cleanly onto the
threadblock-level tiling of CUTLASS/CuTe: a block that is classified
as \enquote{skip} simply does not enter the loop nest of
Algorithm~\ref{alg:swfa}. This observation is what allows SWFA to
deliver measurable speedups without a custom Tensor-Core ISA; the
same pattern has been applied to strided sparsity in Sparse
Transformer~\cite{child2019generating} and to the global-plus-random
mask of BigBird~\cite{zaheer2020bigbird}, but---to our
knowledge---never combined with the online softmax of Flash Attention
inside a single published kernel.

\section{LLM Serving Systems and KV-Cache Management}\label{sec:lit_serving}

Serving workloads differ qualitatively from training: requests arrive
asynchronously, prompts share large prefixes, and the decode phase is
memory-bound rather than compute-bound. A single chat request may
contribute a few hundred tokens of unique suffix on top of a
multi-thousand-token system prompt shared with every other concurrent
request; the serving runtime that fails to exploit that prefix
sharing pays a linear cost in the shared part.
vLLM~\cite{vllm} was the first widely-used runtime to treat the KV
cache as a pool of fixed-size pages and to introduce \emph{continuous
batching}, in which new requests can join the batch at every decode
step. SGLang~\cite{sglang} builds on this with RadixAttention and a
structured-generation language that compiles complex prompt flows into
a scheduled dataflow of kernel calls. Both systems stress the kernel
layer in new ways---RadixAttention changes the access pattern of the
attention kernel, and continuous batching produces many small, tightly
scheduled kernel launches.

The kernel-level contributions of Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang} are placed
in this lineage. In contrast to the system-level papers that describe
vLLM and SGLang, this thesis focuses on the \emph{individual kernels}
that the runtime dispatches to, and reports their performance in
isolation so that improvements can be attributed to specific kernel
changes.

\section{GPU Kernel Profiling and Auto-tuning}\label{sec:lit_profiling}

Kernel-level performance analysis has historically drawn on two
complementary traditions. Vendor metric collectors---Nsight
Compute~\cite{nsight} on NVIDIA silicon, and \texttt{nvprof} before
it---expose occupancy, cache hit rates, and the instruction-mix
breakdown needed to localise a stall to a specific hardware unit.
The roofline model~\cite{williams2009roofline} complements this with
an analytical upper bound: the sustained throughput of a kernel is
capped by either the peak compute rate or by the product of its
arithmetic intensity and the memory bandwidth, whichever comes
first. For an A100 operating in FP16 ($312$\,TFLOPS of Tensor-Core
throughput against $1{,}555$\,GB/s of HBM2e bandwidth), the two caps
cross at an intensity of roughly $200$\,FLOP per byte. Dense
square GEMMs comfortably exceed that threshold and therefore live
on the compute ceiling; attention-score GEMMs with small
$d_{\text{head}}$, and single-query decode kernels, sit well below
it and are bandwidth-limited by design.

Triton's built-in \texttt{autotune} mechanism iterates over block
shapes, warp counts, and pipeline depths and caches the best
configuration per input shape. CUTLASS has a similar
\texttt{GemmUniversal} configuration enumerator and an external
\texttt{cutlass\_profiler} binary. The open literature contains many
single-kernel auto-tuning studies but few end-to-end pipelines that
cross-validate Triton, CUTLASS, and Torch-inductor on the same LLM
kernels. Chapter~\ref{ch:triton} presents such a pipeline.

\section{Energy and Carbon Considerations}\label{sec:lit_energy}
Patterson~et~al.~\cite{patterson2021carbon} estimate that training GPT-3
emitted about $552$\,t\,CO\textsubscript{2}e, equivalent to
$115$ transatlantic passenger flights, and that the serving cost
accumulated over the deployment lifetime can exceed the one-off training
cost. This motivates an energy-aware comparison of distributed training
strategies (Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed}) and an explicit accounting of
kWh per epoch, which appears in our results tables alongside the
traditional throughput and memory metrics.

\section{Cross-Cutting Concerns}\label{sec:lit_cross}

Three concerns cut across the five layers above and therefore deserve
a brief treatment before the positioning section.

\subsection{Numerical Precision at
Scale}\label{sec:lit_precision}
Mixed-precision training (Section~\ref{sec:lit_mp_amp}) is only safe
if every kernel in the stack agrees on the accumulator precision.
CUTLASS and Triton both allow the accumulator to be specified
independently of the input data type, and the distributed-training
frameworks (DDP, FSDP, DeepSpeed) expose a \texttt{reduce\_dtype} that
keeps the cross-device reduction in FP32 even when the parameters live
in FP16. This thesis follows the same convention at every layer: FP16
or BF16 inputs, FP32 accumulators, and FP32 reductions. The numerical
validation in Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} confirms that this is sufficient
to match a vanilla PyTorch reference within
$10^{-4}$ FP32 and $10^{-2}$ FP16 tolerance. Looking forward, FP8 on
Hopper~\cite{nvidia_hopper} and emerging FP4/NVFP6 formats will push
the accumulator-precision question into the epilogue rather than the
main MMA loop; the CUTLASS epilogue templates of
Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass} are structurally ready for this transition,
though the shape-indexed auto-tune cache of
Chapter~\ref{ch:triton} needs widening to include the
accumulator-precision axis.

\subsection{Reproducibility in a Non-Deterministic
Stack}\label{sec:lit_repro}
Every layer of the stack is non-deterministic by default: PyTorch's
own reduction kernels choose the reduction order based on the launch
configuration, NCCL selects between ring and tree algorithms based on
message size, and continuous batching in SGLang produces
launch orders that depend on the live request mix. A literal
bit-level reproduction is therefore impossible. This thesis instead
follows the convention of \emph{statistical} reproducibility: fixed
random seeds, fixed hardware, and a reported relative standard
deviation below $1\%$ on every measured configuration. Where a
deterministic path exists---most notably for the numerical-accuracy
validation of SWFA (Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa})---it is used, and the
deterministic output is compared bit-exactly to the PyTorch reference.

\subsection{Energy as a First-Class
Metric}\label{sec:lit_energy_metric}
Following Patterson~et~al.~\cite{patterson2021carbon}, this thesis
reports energy per epoch alongside throughput at every layer where it
can be measured. The argument for doing so is not primarily
environmental: at data-centre scale, energy is simply a proxy for
the sum of idle-time, memory-stall, and under-utilised-compute costs,
none of which show up in a raw throughput number. A training run
that finishes in $8$ hours but consumes $20\%$ more energy than
necessary has $20\%$ of wasted work sitting somewhere in the stack,
and knowing which layer contains that waste is the purpose of a
controlled comparison (Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed}) plus a
cross-validating profiling pipeline (Chapter~\ref{ch:triton}).

\section{Positioning of This Thesis}\label{sec:lit_gap}

The picture that emerges from the literature is one of rapid progress
in each of the five areas---distributed training, GEMM libraries,
efficient attention, serving runtimes, and kernel profiling---but with
limited integration across them. Distributed-training studies rarely
report kernel-level measurements; attention-kernel papers are usually
benchmarked in isolation; and serving-system papers tend to treat the
attention kernel as a fixed dependency. This thesis takes the
complementary view that the training, kernel, attention, serving, and
profiling layers must be co-designed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a sliding-window attention kernel only pays off if the serving
          runtime exposes the window to it, and if the underlying GEMM
          library can efficiently launch the resulting batched and
          sparse tiles;
    \item a distributed-training schedule only reaches peak efficiency
          if the underlying GEMM and attention kernels are well-tuned;
          unused Tensor Cores at the kernel level are simply inherited
          at every scale of parallelism;
    \item a profiling pipeline that reports the same metric across
          Triton, CUTLASS, and PyTorch is the only way to reason
          quantitatively about these interactions without being
          mis-led by per-library calibration differences.
\end{itemize}
Chapters~\ref{ch:distributed}--\ref{ch:triton} develop these layers in
turn and show in the evaluation that they compose.
