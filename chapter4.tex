\chapter{CUTLASS and CuTe GEMM Operations}\label{ch:cutlass}

\emph{Placement in the stack.} Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed} established
the per-GPU memory envelope under each distributed strategy. Inside
that envelope, the wall-clock time of a training step is dominated by
a handful of GEMM shapes that every transformer layer issues at every
step. This chapter characterises those shapes and shows how to drive
each of them with a CUTLASS/CuTe kernel that reaches near-peak
Tensor-Core utilisation. The epilogue-fusion primitives developed
here are reused by the attention-output writeback of
Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} and the logit-projection GEMM of
Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}, so the numbers in this chapter also bound
what those higher layers can achieve.

Matrix multiplication (GEMM) dominates the runtime of modern
transformer-based LLMs: every projection layer, MLP block, and
attention score/output computation reduces to a GEMM (or a
batched/grouped variant). This chapter describes how high-performance
GEMM kernels are structured using NVIDIA's CUTLASS library and the CuTe
layout algebra, which form the kernel layer of the proposed software
stack.

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:cutlass_motivation}
While PyTorch dispatches most matrix multiplications to cuBLAS,
production LLM workloads require custom GEMM variants that cuBLAS does
not expose directly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grouped and variable-shape GEMM} for Mixture-of-Experts
          layers, where each expert has its own $(M_e, N_e, K)$ shape
          and the launch scheduler must pack all experts into a single
          kernel invocation;
    \item \textbf{Quantised GEMM} (INT8/FP8/INT4) for inference, where
          mixed-precision accumulators and dequantisation epilogues are
          required;
    \item \textbf{Epilogue fusion} (bias, GELU, residual add, LayerNorm)
          to reduce memory traffic by avoiding a second pass over the
          output tensor;
    \item \textbf{Fused attention} where softmax and value multiplication
          are co-designed with the score GEMM
          (FlashAttention~\cite{dao2022flashattention}).
\end{itemize}
CUTLASS~\cite{cutlass} provides a C++ template library for writing
such kernels at the level of Tensor Cores, and CuTe is its underlying
layout and tensor algebra. Together they strike a balance between
cuBLAS's opacity (hard to extend, impossible to fuse) and raw CUDA
(easy to mis-use, tedious to retarget across architectures).

\section{The CUTLASS Programming Model}\label{sec:cutlass_model}
CUTLASS decomposes a GEMM $C \leftarrow \alpha\, A B + \beta\, C$ into
a hierarchy of nested tiles that map onto the GPU execution hierarchy:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\textbf{Level} & \textbf{GPU abstraction} \\
\toprule
Threadblock tile & Cooperative Thread Array (CTA) / shared memory \\
Warp tile        & Warp (32 threads) \\
Instruction tile & Tensor Core MMA (e.g., \texttt{mma.m16n8k16}) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For input matrices $A \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times K}$ and
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}$, the kernel:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item partitions $C$ into threadblock tiles of size
          $\mathtt{Bm} \times \mathtt{Bn}$;
    \item each CTA iterates over the $K$ dimension in chunks of
          $\mathtt{Bk}$, cooperatively loading $A$ and $B$ tiles into
          shared memory;
    \item warps read from shared memory into registers and issue MMA
          instructions, accumulating into an FP32/FP16 register tile;
    \item an \emph{epilogue} writes the result back to global memory,
          optionally fusing bias, activation, and residual adds.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tile Sizes on A100}\label{sec:cutlass_tile_sizes}
Tile sizes are a compromise between data reuse and register/shared-memory
occupancy. On A100 we typically use:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\textbf{GEMM shape class}    & \textbf{Threadblock} & \textbf{Warp} \\
\toprule
Large square / MLP           & $128 \times 128 \times 32$ & $64 \times 64 \times 32$ \\
Skinny (attention score)     & $128 \times 64  \times 32$ & $64 \times 32 \times 32$ \\
Very skinny (decode attn)    & $128 \times 16  \times 32$ & $64 \times 16 \times 32$ \\
Grouped (MoE)                & $128 \times 128 \times 32$ & $64 \times 64 \times 32$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The warp tile is chosen so that each warp holds an integer number of
$16 \times 8 \times 16$ MMA fragments in registers without spilling.

\section{Software Pipelining}\label{sec:cutlass_pipelining}
Hiding the HBM latency is achieved through multi-stage \emph{software
pipelining}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hopper/Ampere provide asynchronous copy instructions
          (\texttt{cp.async} on Ampere, TMA on Hopper) that fetch the
          next $K$-tile while the current one is being consumed by MMA
          instructions;
    \item CUTLASS uses $\geq 3$ shared-memory buffers to keep the copy
          engine, the MMA unit, and the epilogue fully utilised;
    \item swizzled layouts ensure conflict-free shared-memory accesses
          for the MMA load patterns;
    \item \texttt{cp.async} commit/wait groups are exposed via CUTLASS's
          \texttt{MmaMultistage} class, which enqueues $N_{\text{stage}}$
          fetches ahead of the current MMA issue.
\end{itemize}

The effective HBM latency $\tau_{\text{HBM}}$ seen by each GEMM step is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \max\bigl(\tau_{\text{HBM}},
            N_{\text{stage}} \cdot \tau_{\text{MMA}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
so with $N_{\text{stage}} = 3$ and the A100's $\tau_{\text{MMA}}
\approx 2$\,cycles per $16\times 8 \times 16$ MMA, we hide latencies up
to $\sim\!6$\,cycles per MMA---sufficient to absorb L2 hits and shared-memory
conflicts but still short of the $\sim\!200$\,cycles of an HBM miss, which
the pipeline fetches overlap one stage earlier.

\section{CuTe Layouts}\label{sec:cute_layouts}
CuTe~\cite{cutlass} models a tensor as a pair
\[
    T = (\text{shape},\text{stride}),
\]
where \emph{shape} is a (nested) tuple describing the coordinate space
and \emph{stride} a tuple describing the mapping from coordinates to a
flat index. A simple row-major $M\times N$ matrix has layout
$(\text{shape} = (M, N), \text{stride} = (N, 1))$.

\subsection{Composition}\label{sec:cute_composition}
The central operation is \texttt{Layout} composition: given layouts
$L_A, L_B$, the composite $L_A \circ L_B$ reinterprets the coordinates
of $L_B$ through $L_A$. For example, composing a row-major layout of
shape $(8, 8)$ with a swizzle layout permutes the columns to avoid
$32$-way shared-memory bank conflicts. This enables concise
descriptions of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item tiled partitions of $A$ and $B$ (row-major, column-major,
          blocked, swizzled);
    \item warp- and thread-level partitioning of a shared-memory tile;
    \item the register-level fragment shapes required by each MMA
          instruction variant.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hierarchical Tiling}\label{sec:cute_tiling}
A single transformer GEMM can be expressed as a chain of
\texttt{local\_tile} operations:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize]
// A: (M, K) with layout mA, tile into (BLK_M, BLK_K) per CTA
auto gA = local_tile(mA, make_shape(BLK_M, BLK_K),
                     make_coord(blockIdx.x, _));
// Warp-level partition within the CTA tile
auto tAsA = thrd_partition_A(warp_mma, gA);
// Copy to shared memory with an async-copy layout
auto tAgA = copy(copy_atom_cp_async, tAgA, tAsA);
\end{lstlisting}
The benefit is that a single kernel template can be instantiated for
many data types, tile shapes, and hardware generations simply by
changing the layout parameters; the actual MMA instruction selection is
done by the \texttt{Sm80} / \texttt{Sm90} traits class.

\section{Mapping LLM Layers onto GEMM}\label{sec:cutlass_llm}
For a transformer layer with hidden size $d_{\text{model}}$,
intermediate size $d_{\text{ff}}$, batch $B$, and sequence length $N$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{QKV projection.} Three GEMMs of shape
          $(BN)\times d_{\text{model}} \cdot d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{head}}$;
          in practice fused as a single GEMM of shape
          $(BN)\times d_{\text{model}} \cdot d_{\text{model}}\times 3d_{\text{head}}$.
          A bias-add epilogue removes one HBM pass over the QKV tensor.
    \item \textbf{Attention score.} Batched GEMM
          $Q\cdot K^{\!\top}$ of shape
          $N\times d_{\text{head}} \cdot d_{\text{head}}\times N$ per
          head. This shape is skinny in $d_{\text{head}}$ (typically
          $64$ or $128$), which is why cuBLAS underperforms here.
    \item \textbf{Attention output.} Batched GEMM of the softmaxed
          scores with $V$, $N\times N \cdot N\times d_{\text{head}}$.
          FlashAttention fuses both attention GEMMs and the softmax in
          a single kernel driven by CuTe tiles.
    \item \textbf{MLP.} Two GEMMs of shapes
          $(BN)\times d_{\text{model}}\cdot d_{\text{model}}\times d_{\text{ff}}$
          and
          $(BN)\times d_{\text{ff}}\cdot d_{\text{ff}}\times d_{\text{model}}$,
          with a GELU epilogue fused into the first.
    \item \textbf{MoE.} A grouped GEMM of $E$ experts, each with its
          own $(M_e, N_e, K)$ shape drawn from the routing histogram.
          CUTLASS's \texttt{GemmGrouped} enqueues all groups into a
          single kernel launch, which amortises the launch overhead
          over the $E$ experts.
\end{itemize}
CUTLASS provides templated kernels for all of the above, including
\texttt{GemmGrouped} for MoE and \texttt{AttentionKernel} for
Flash-Attention-style fusion.

\section{Epilogue Fusion}\label{sec:cutlass_epilogue}
A large fraction of kernel calls in an LLM are element-wise operations
(bias, GELU, residual add, RMSNorm). These are memory-bound in
isolation. CUTLASS epilogues let us fold them into the preceding GEMM's
writeback:
\begin{equation}
    C \;\leftarrow\; \phi\!\left(\alpha\, A B +
                                 \beta\, C_{\text{prev}} +
                                 \text{bias}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is an activation function (e.g., GELU, SiLU). This removes
one pass over $C$ from HBM and is typically worth $10$--$25\%$
end-to-end throughput on an A100.

\subsection{Fused Bias+GELU
Epilogue}\label{sec:cutlass_bias_gelu}
A bias+GELU epilogue in CUTLASS is:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize]
using Epilogue = cutlass::epilogue::thread::LinearCombinationGELU<
    ElementOutput,        // FP16
    128 / cutlass::sizeof_bits<ElementOutput>::value,
    ElementAccumulator,   // FP32
    ElementCompute>;      // FP32
\end{lstlisting}
The crucial point is that $\beta \cdot C_{\text{prev}} + \text{bias}$
is evaluated in FP32 in the epilogue's register tile before the GELU,
preserving numerical accuracy even when $C$ is stored in FP16.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:cutlass_method}

\subsection{Kernels Studied}\label{sec:cutlass_method_kernels}
To stress the design space that transformer workloads actually
exercise, we benchmark four shape classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a large square GEMM ($4096\!\times\!4096\!\times\!4096$)
          representative of MLP projections at a modest batch;
    \item a larger square GEMM ($8192\!\times\!8192\!\times\!8192$)
          that saturates Tensor Cores and is used as the reference for
          peak FLOPS;
    \item a skinny GEMM ($2048\!\times\!2048\!\times\!128$)
          representative of attention score and output matmuls per head;
    \item a grouped GEMM for a Mixture-of-Experts layer with $16$
          experts, each with local shape
          $(M_e, N_e, K) = (512, 4096, 4096)$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Implementations Compared}\label{sec:cutlass_method_impl}
Each kernel is run in three implementations: cuBLAS (vendor baseline),
CUTLASS 3.x with tuned tile shapes, and---where applicable---a
hand-rolled CuTe kernel with an epilogue-fused bias+GELU. The same
input tensors (FP16 weights, FP32 accumulators) are used across
implementations so that only the kernel differs.

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:cutlass_method_metrics}
For every kernel we report sustained throughput (TFLOPS), the ratio
against cuBLAS, and Tensor-Core utilisation as reported by Nsight
Compute. Latencies are measured with CUDA events after a fixed warm-up
of $25$ iterations, and each configuration is repeated until the
relative standard deviation falls below $1\%$. The TFLOPS figure uses
the $2 M N K$ convention, counting each multiply--add as two FLOPs.

\subsection{Hardware and Software}\label{sec:cutlass_method_hw}
All numbers are on a single A100-40GB, CUDA 11.8 and CUTLASS 3.2 with
the driver pinned to a single PCIe topology so that the numbers are
reproducible across runs. ECC is enabled and the GPU clock is fixed at
the Tensor-Core boost frequency to eliminate clock-frequency variance.

\subsection{Epilogue Fusion End-to-End}\label{sec:cutlass_fusion_e2e}
When the bias+GELU epilogue is fused into FC1 and the residual-add
epilogue into FC2, the end-to-end GPT-2 Medium MLP block runs $18\%$
faster at $N = 2048$, batch $4$, and $22\%$ faster at $N = 4096$,
batch $4$. The savings come entirely from the eliminated HBM passes in
Table~\ref{tab:epilogue_bw}.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:cutlass_summary}
CUTLASS and CuTe provide the low-level GEMM primitives that the rest
of this thesis builds upon: the Sliding Window Flash Attention kernel
of Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} and the SGLang serving kernels of
Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang} are natural clients of these abstractions. The
principal insight of the chapter is that skinny and grouped GEMMs---%
the shapes that dominate transformer attention and MoE---benefit
substantially from a template library that exposes the tile hierarchy
explicitly, and that epilogue fusion recovers a further
$10$--$25\%$ of end-to-end throughput that would otherwise be lost to
HBM round-trips.
