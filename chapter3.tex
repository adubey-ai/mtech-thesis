\section{Background}\label{sec:dist_bg}

\subsection{Formal Setting}\label{sec:dist_formal}
Write $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^M$ for the full parameter vector of
the model, $f_\theta$ for the loss on a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$ of
size $B$, and $P$ for the number of workers. A generic distributed
training step then reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dist_step}
    g \;=\; \frac{1}{P} \sum_{p=1}^{P}
            \nabla_\theta f_\theta(\mathcal{B}_p),
    \qquad
    \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \,g,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_p \subset \mathcal{B}$ is the slice that
worker~$p$ consumes. What distinguishes the four strategies
compared in this chapter is only two things: (i)~which portions of
$\theta$, $g$, and the optimiser state each worker chooses to
store, and (ii)~which collective operations are used to keep
equation~\eqref{eq:dist_step} numerically equivalent to the single
device computation.

\subsection{Data Parallelism}\label{sec:dp_bg}
Of all distributed-training schemes, data parallelism is the
oldest. Every worker hosts a full replica of the model, takes a
disjoint slice of the data batch, computes its gradients on that
slice locally, and then enters an all-reduce so that every replica
observes the same synchronised gradient before the optimiser step
fires. The plain form of data parallelism---often just called
\emph{DP}---is easy to implement but suffers on two fronts: the
communication cost of sending a full gradient every step, and the
memory cost of storing the whole model on every worker.

PyTorch's DDP path~\cite{li2020pytorch} attacks both weaknesses at
once. Gradients are collected into fixed-size \emph{buckets}, each
bucket issues its own all-reduce, and bucket~$k$ can therefore
begin its reduction while bucket~$k+1$ is still being produced by
the backward pass. The reductions ride on a ring topology whose
per-step bandwidth is
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{DDP}} \;=\; \frac{2(P-1)}{P} \cdot M \cdot s_{\text{grad}} ,
\end{equation}
with $s_{\text{grad}}$ the size of a gradient element. Because the
leading factor $2(P-1)/P$ converges to $2$ for any non-trivial $P$,
the per-worker wire traffic is essentially independent of the world
size---and it is precisely that scaling property that has made ring
all-reduce the preferred collective at cluster scale.

\subsection{Model Parallelism and Sharding}\label{sec:mp_bg}
Once the model has outgrown a single accelerator, some form of
\emph{model parallelism} becomes unavoidable. Two classical variants
dominate the literature:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pipeline parallelism}: successive stages of the
          model live on different devices. Pipeline bubbles and
          load balancing between stages remain non-trivial.
          GPipe~\cite{huang2019gpipe} amortises the bubble by
          running $m$ micro-batches concurrently through the
          pipeline and brings the bubble fraction down to
          $(P-1)/(m+P-1)$.
    \item \textbf{Tensor parallelism}: individual layers are split
          across devices, as popularised by
          Megatron-LM~\cite{shoeybi2019megatron}. Each partitioned
          layer inserts one all-reduce into the forward pass and a
          second into the backward pass, and the approach works
          best inside a single NVLink domain.
\end{itemize}
A more recent approach, \emph{Fully Sharded Data Parallel}
(FSDP)~\cite{zhao2023pytorch}, fuses data parallelism with
parameter sharding. Parameters, gradients, and optimiser states
are distributed across every device in the group; for each layer
the parameters are all-gathered just in time, used for the forward
or backward pass, and then immediately re-sharded.

\subsection{ZeRO and DeepSpeed}\label{sec:zero_bg}
ZeRO~\cite{rajbhandari2019zero,rajbhandari2021zero} generalises the
data-parallel template by asking a single question for each piece
of the training state: which workers really need to keep a copy of
it, and at which point during the step? The answer for the Adam
moments is given by stage one: the moments are only consulted
during the optimiser step, so one shard per worker is enough, and a
reduce-scatter produces that shard in place. Stage two extends the
argument to the gradients, eliminating the full gradient tensor
from every worker's memory. Stage three pushes it all the way to
the parameters themselves: the parameters now live sharded, and
each layer gathers the missing slices on demand just before it
executes. At stage three the per-worker memory scales as $M/P$,
which is the lower bound that plain DP never approaches; the price
paid is two extra collective phases per layer, and those phases
have to be hidden by the overlap scheduling discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:res_overlap} or the throughput regresses.
The production implementation of ZeRO is
DeepSpeed~\cite{rasley2020deepspeed}, which wraps the core idea
together with gradient compression, dynamic loss scaling, CPU and
NVMe offloading, and Mixture-of-Experts
support~\cite{rajbhandari2022deepspeed}. Concretely, ZeRO-3 issues
two collectives per layer over and above DDP---an all-gather before
the forward or backward pass and a reduce-scatter after the
backward pass---and each of them has to be overlapped carefully
with compute if a throughput regression is to be avoided.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:dist_method}

\subsection{Training Strategies and Metrics}\label{sec:dist_method_metrics}
The four strategies chosen for comparison span the memory and
throughput design space:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DP}---the full-replication baseline;
    \item \textbf{DDP}---the industry-standard form of multi-GPU
          data parallelism;
    \item \textbf{FSDP}---data parallelism with parameter sharding;
    \item \textbf{DeepSpeed ZeRO-3}---fully sharded, with optional
          CPU/NVMe offload.
\end{itemize}
Each of them is then assessed along several axes: GPU utilisation
in percent, peak and average per-GPU memory in GB, per-epoch
training-loss convergence, average and peak power draw in watts
together with the per-epoch energy in kWh, communication overhead,
and scaling efficiency.

\subsection{Workload}\label{sec:dist_method_workload}
Two workloads are used, each of which stresses a different regime.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Controlled benchmark.} ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10
          ($25.6$M parameters), a widely reproducible setup that
          swings between compute-bound and communication-bound
          behaviour as the batch size and worker count are varied.
          This is the main workload for the GPU-utilisation,
          memory, and training-loss tables.
    \item \textbf{LLM benchmark.} GPT-2 Medium ($355$M parameters),
          trained on a subset of OpenWebText with sequence length
          $2048$, a context window of $w = 1024$, and a per-GPU
          micro-batch of four. This workload supplies the
          end-to-end LLM throughput numbers when combined with the
          SWFA kernel of Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa}.
\end{itemize}
The random seed ($42$) and the optimiser hyper-parameters (Adam,
$\eta = 3 \times 10^{-4}$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$,
$\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$) are identical across all four strategies,
so that any divergence in the final loss can be attributed to the
strategy itself and nothing else.

\subsection{Hardware and Software}\label{sec:dist_method_hw}
All experiments run on a single node whose configuration is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 8$\times$ NVIDIA A100 (40\,GB HBM2e each), connected by
          NVLink-3 with $600$\,GB/s of bisection bandwidth;
    \item two Intel Xeon Platinum 8358 host CPUs
          (64 cores, 128 threads total) backed by 1\,TB of DDR4;
    \item PyTorch 2.0.1 with CUDA 11.8, NCCL 2.18.1, and DeepSpeed
          0.9.5;
    \item NVIDIA driver $535.104.05$, GCC 11.4, and CUTLASS 3.2.
\end{itemize}
Power and energy numbers are read off the out-of-band BMC
interface; GPU utilisation and per-GPU memory are sampled from
\texttt{nvidia-smi} every $100$\,ms and aggregated per epoch.

\section{Implementations}\label{sec:dist_impl}

\subsection{DP}\label{sec:impl_dp}
The DP baseline uses \texttt{torch.nn.DataParallel}, which
replicates the model onto every GPU, scatters non-overlapping
slices of the data, performs the local forward and backward, and
synchronises via \texttt{all\_reduce} immediately before each
optimiser step. DP is conceptually transparent, but its throughput
is held back by three factors: the communication cost of the full
gradient, the memory cost of the replicated model, and the fact
that a single Python thread is responsible for coordinating every
worker.

\subsection{DDP}\label{sec:impl_ddp}
DDP offers the same user-facing programming model as DP, but
internally it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item partitions the gradients into communication \emph{buckets}
          of (by default) $25$\,MB so that the all-reduce
          granularity lines up with the granularity at which the
          backward pass produces them;
    \item overlaps the reduction of the earlier buckets with the
          backward pass of the later layers, using a per-parameter
          hook;
    \item routes those reductions around a ring all-reduce, which
          is the collective that saturates NVLink;
    \item hooks autograd's \texttt{reducer} into the CUDA stream so
          that communication and compute occupy non-default streams.
\end{itemize}
The PyTorch configuration is a one-liner:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
import torch.distributed as dist
dist.init_process_group(backend="nccl")
model = torch.nn.parallel.DistributedDataParallel(
    model.to(local_rank), device_ids=[local_rank],
    bucket_cap_mb=25, find_unused_parameters=False)
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{FSDP}\label{sec:impl_fsdp}
In FSDP every wrapped unit has its parameter tensor split across
devices,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Shard}_i = \{\theta_j : j \bmod P = i\},
\end{equation}
with $P$ the world size and $i$ the rank of the worker. Before the
unit runs its forward or backward, FSDP issues an
\emph{all-gather} that reassembles the full parameter; once the
gradient has been computed, it issues a \emph{reduce-scatter} and
re-shards the result. The memory saving comes directly from the
fact that no worker ever holds the complete model at once. The
sharding unit chosen here is the transformer block itself, wrapped
via \texttt{auto\_wrap\_policy}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
from torch.distributed.fsdp import FullyShardedDataParallel as FSDP
from torch.distributed.fsdp.wrap import transformer_auto_wrap_policy
policy = functools.partial(
    transformer_auto_wrap_policy,
    transformer_layer_cls={TransformerBlock})
model = FSDP(model, auto_wrap_policy=policy,
             mixed_precision=fp16_policy,
             sharding_strategy=ShardingStrategy.FULL_SHARD,
             device_id=local_rank)
\end{lstlisting}
A transformer with $L$ layers therefore costs $L$
all-gather/reduce-scatter pairs per step, which is precisely the
reason the unit of sharding has to be aligned with the unit of
compute.

\subsection{DeepSpeed ZeRO-3}\label{sec:impl_ds}
DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 augments parameter sharding with an efficient
communication scheduler and with optional offload of parameters
and optimiser state to CPU or NVMe. Mixed precision (FP16 or
BF16), gradient compression, and MoE support are all provided
out of the box. Configuration happens through a JSON file:
\begin{lstlisting}[basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize]
{
  "zero_optimization": {
    "stage": 3,
    "overlap_comm": true,
    "contiguous_gradients": true,
    "stage3_prefetch_bucket_size": 2e8,
    "stage3_param_persistence_threshold": 1e6
  },
  "fp16": { "enabled": true, "loss_scale_window": 1000 },
  "train_micro_batch_size_per_gpu": 4,
  "gradient_accumulation_steps": 1
}
\end{lstlisting}
The prefetch bucket governs how eagerly parameters for the next
layer are gathered. Setting it too large saturates NVLink
bandwidth on ZeRO-3; setting it too small leaves compute units
idle waiting for the next all-gather.

\section{Discussion and Recommendations}\label{sec:dist_discussion}

\subsection{Strategy Choice}\label{sec:dist_choice}
On the basis of the results above, the recommendation is:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Large models ($>$1\,B parameters).} DeepSpeed
          ZeRO-3 is the primary recommendation. FSDP is a close
          alternative with a cleaner PyTorch-native deployment
          story and fewer moving parts in the build.
    \item \textbf{Medium models ($100$\,M--$1$\,B).} DDP offers
          the best trade-off between performance and complexity.
          FSDP is to be preferred when memory is tight or when
          longer sequences inflate the activation footprint.
    \item \textbf{Small models ($<$100\,M).} DDP is already
          sufficient. DP remains useful only for debugging and
          for first-time bring-up.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{When Overlap Wins}\label{sec:dist_overlap_wins}
The overlap figures in Table~\ref{tab:overlap} explain why DDP
falls only marginally behind FSDP and DeepSpeed in GPU utilisation
despite carrying a larger memory footprint. The ring all-reduce is
the most compute-friendly collective available on NVLink, and
bucket-level scheduling is enough to keep the wire busy. The
sharded strategies do pay a latency premium for the extra
all-gather, but they more than recover it through the roughly
$8\times$ memory headroom, which in turn enables larger batches and
a better balance between compute and memory pressure.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:dist_limitations}
This study is restricted to a single NVLink-connected node.
Multi-node scaling brings an InfiniBand layer into the picture
whose latency characteristics would tilt the balance toward
strategies that minimise cross-node traffic---pipeline parallelism,
gradient compression, and 2-D sharding schemes such as Megatron-3D.
These directions are noted as future work in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:dist_summary}
The chapter presented a controlled comparison of four distributed
training strategies on a common hardware configuration and a common
workload. The sharded strategies---FSDP and ZeRO-3---dominate along
every axis except raw simplicity, and DeepSpeed's aggressive
prefetching just edges out FSDP on NVLink. All four strategies rest
on the same kernel primitives (GEMMs and attention), and that is
exactly what motivates the next two chapters: any inefficiency in
the kernel layer is inherited by every training schedule at every
scale.
