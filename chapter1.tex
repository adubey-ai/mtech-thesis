\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\section{Background and Motivation}\label{sec:intro_background}
Within only a few years, Large Language Models---all of them
descendants of the transformer design~\cite{vaswani2017attention}---have
shifted from an interesting research artefact to the workload that
dominates most newly installed supercomputing clusters. When one
traces the path from the $175$-billion parameters of
GPT-3~\cite{brown2020gpt3}, through the $540$-billion of
PaLM~\cite{chowdhery2022palm}, and into the open-weight LLaMA
line~\cite{touvron2023llama,touvron2023llama2}, each new model is
not so much a qualitative leap as a direct reading of the compute-loss
scaling relationships~\cite{kaplan2020scaling,hoffmann2022chinchilla}:
whatever the compute budget, the model that best minimises the loss
turns out to be bigger, and fed more tokens, than the previous
generation of folklore had assumed. From an engineering point of view,
the implication is painful: every generation tightens the
per-device memory budget, raises the communication volume demanded
of the cluster, and stretches the context lengths on which attention
has to be computed~\cite{rajbhandari2019zero,shoeybi2019megatron}. A
stack that sufficed for the previous generation almost never suffices
for the next one without being retuned from top to bottom.

\subsection{A Concrete Memory Accounting}\label{sec:intro_memory_example}
A short back-of-the-envelope calculation is enough to make the
constraints concrete. Consider a $7$-billion-parameter transformer
trained in FP16 mixed precision with Adam. Each parameter then costs
$2$\,B for the weight itself, another $2$\,B for its gradient, and
$4 \cdot 2$\,B more for the FP32 master copy together with the two
moment buffers that Adam keeps---$14$\,B per parameter in total, or
about $98$\,GB merely for the persistent model state. That figure
already exceeds the $40$\,GB HBM of a single A100, and it ignores
activations, which at a sequence of $N{=}2048$ and a batch of $B{=}4$
contribute roughly a further $20$\,GB per transformer layer. Scaling
to a $70$-billion-parameter model enlarges the state budget by a factor
of ten and the activation budget by about three---both $d_{\text{model}}$
and the depth climb. Memory, in other words, is the first-order
constraint on \emph{what can be trained at all}, and it is precisely
the constraint that determines every sharding decision made in this
thesis.

\subsection{The Four Challenges That Motivate This Thesis}\label{sec:intro_four_challenges}

Today's LLMs demand an amount of hardware that no single GPU can
supply. Systems such as PaLM or LLaMA-3 cannot be trained at all
without spreading the job across hundreds, sometimes thousands, of
accelerators. The moment one steps beyond a single device, four
mutually coupled difficulties appear at once:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Memory management.} Even a $7$-billion parameter
          model, once its weights, gradients, and optimiser state are
          counted, overruns the $40$\,GB HBM available on one A100.
          Adam-style updates~\cite{rasley2020deepspeed} attach two
          extra tensors---the first and second moment estimates---to
          every parameter, effectively tripling the parameter storage,
          and mixed-precision recipes~\cite{micikevicius2018amp}
          additionally demand a separate FP32 master copy.
    \item \textbf{Communication overhead.} The cost of synchronising
          gradients across a data-parallel group is a function of the
          model's size rather than the batch's; unless communication
          is deliberately overlapped with compute, the accelerators
          sit idle waiting on NVLink or InfiniBand for most of the
          step.
    \item \textbf{Hardware heterogeneity.} A production stack is
          expected to run on a heterogeneous fleet---A100, H100,
          Intel Xe, AMD CDNA---each with its own memory hierarchy,
          its own Tensor-Core or matrix-engine ISA, and its own
          interconnect topology, so that the same training job can
          behave very differently depending on the SKU it happens
          to land on.
    \item \textbf{Attention efficiency.} Self-attention contributes
          $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ work and, at long context, dominates the
          overall FLOP count. Masking an element-wise sparse pattern
          after the fact is not enough: the underlying dense GEMM is
          still dispatched over blocks whose outputs are later
          discarded.
\end{itemize}

On top of all this sits the energy and carbon footprint of
training~\cite{patterson2021carbon}. The accounting here is almost
linear: a $10\%$ gain in compute utilisation translates more or less
directly into a $10\%$ saving in electricity and embodied carbon. Any
optimisation that cuts wall-clock time and energy per epoch
simultaneously therefore has value that extends well beyond its raw
speedup.

\subsection{A Stack View of the Problem}\label{sec:intro_stack}
In what follows, LLM efficiency is never treated as a single
algorithmic problem. It is treated as a \emph{stack} of interdependent
layers, each of which has to be tuned with one eye on the layer
above it and the other on the layer below:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributed schedule} (Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed}).
          Decides which parts of the model, gradients, and optimiser
          state live on which GPU, and which collective operations keep
          them synchronised. Sets the memory envelope for every kernel
          call below it.
    \item \textbf{Kernel layer} (Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass}). Provides the
          dense GEMMs, grouped GEMMs, and epilogue-fused primitives on
          which every transformer layer is built. Its Tensor-Core
          utilisation is inherited at every scale of parallelism.
    \item \textbf{Attention algorithm} (Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa}).
          Determines the asymptotic complexity of the dominant operator
          at long context. A kernel that ignores the sparsity pattern
          exposed by the model architecture wastes the savings the
          upper layers have worked to create.
    \item \textbf{Serving runtime} (Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}). Turns
          algorithmic savings into end-user-visible throughput by
          scheduling kernel launches, managing the KV cache, and
          batching requests continuously.
    \item \textbf{Profiling and auto-tuning}
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:triton}). Closes the loop: it measures
          whether the four layers above are actually delivering their
          expected performance, and it selects per-shape kernel
          parameters that would otherwise have to be hand-tuned.
\end{enumerate}
Two consequences of this perspective reappear in every chapter.
First, no point optimisation at one layer is more useful than the
next layer up permits it to be---a kernel speedup is wasted if a
slow collective in the distributed schedule serialises everything
behind it. Second, a measurement at one layer is only as reliable
as the independent check performed one layer below it; a
throughput figure at the top can easily conceal a regression in a
kernel that a lower-level profile would have caught immediately.
For these reasons the profiling pipeline described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:triton} is treated as a first-class component of
the contribution, not as a convenient side tool.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{sec:intro_problem}
The questions this thesis sets out to answer are the following.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Scalable distributed training.} The comparisons of
          DP, DDP, FSDP, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 that one finds in the
          published literature are, almost without exception, produced
          by the authors of the individual system, usually on a
          different workload and a different hardware configuration
          each time. What the literature does not contain is a
          controlled single-node study in which the model, the data,
          the seed, and the hardware are all held fixed and only the
          parallelisation strategy is varied.
    \item \textbf{High-performance matrix multiplication.} The GEMM
          kernels in vendor libraries such as cuBLAS are tuned
          aggressively for large, square matrices and behave worse
          for the skinny, batched, and grouped shapes that in fact
          dominate transformer attention and Mixture-of-Experts
          layers. What is needed is a principled mapping of the
          GEMM shapes that LLMs actually produce onto the
          CUTLASS/CuTe abstractions.
    \item \textbf{Attention optimisation.} Sliding-window
          attention~\cite{beltagy2020longformer} has become almost
          universal in long-context models, yet most open
          implementations express the window by applying an
          element-wise mask \emph{after} computing the scores. A
          kernel that recognises the window pattern \emph{before}
          any GEMM is issued would, by contrast, scale near-linearly
          in the window size.
    \item \textbf{Production-grade serving kernels.} LLM inference
          has a different character from training. Requests arrive
          asynchronously, prompts frequently share long prefixes,
          and the decode stage is memory-bound rather than
          compute-bound. Closing the gap between the algorithmic
          savings that are available on paper and the wall-clock
          throughput observed in a running service requires kernel
          work inside the SGLang runtime---a paged KV cache, a
          fused decode attention, and fused sampling primitives.
    \item \textbf{Kernel profiling and tuning.} Every new GPU
          generation and every new input shape invalidates the
          previous best choice of block size, pipeline depth, or
          warp count. A single profiling pipeline that can reason
          across Triton, CUTLASS, and Torch-inductor is therefore
          needed so that these kernel-level decisions do not have
          to be rediscovered from scratch.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Objectives}\label{sec:intro_objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Design and evaluate a modular high-performance deep-learning
          software stack that supports efficient LLM training and
          inference across diverse hardware, with clean interfaces between
          the distributed scheduler, the kernel layer, the attention
          algorithm, and the serving runtime.
    \item Implement and benchmark four distributed training
          strategies---DP, DDP, FSDP, and DeepSpeed ZeRO-3---on an 8-GPU
          server, and report GPU utilisation, per-GPU memory footprint,
          training-loss convergence, power consumption, energy per epoch,
          and scaling efficiency on a single unified workload.
    \item Study CUTLASS/CuTe-based GEMM pipelines and map the principal
          LLM layers (QKV, MLP, attention score, attention output, grouped
          MoE) onto the Tensor-Core hierarchy, reporting Tensor-Core
          utilisation and end-to-end speedups against cuBLAS.
    \item Develop a block-sparse Sliding Window Flash Attention (SWFA)
          algorithm with a rigorous accuracy guarantee, a closed-form
          optimal-window derivation, and a numerical validation against a
          vanilla attention reference over nineteen configurations.
    \item Engineer GPU kernels for the SGLang serving runtime---paged KV
          cache, decode attention, prefill attention, and fused
          sampling---and measure their cumulative effect on LLaMA-7B
          decode throughput.
    \item Build a Triton-based profiling workflow for kernel-level
          analysis that combines CUDA-event timing, roofline analysis,
          and Nsight Compute cross-checks, with an auto-tuner that caches
          configurations keyed by input shape and data type.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contributions}\label{sec:intro_contributions}
The main contributions of this thesis are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Controlled distributed-training study.} A systematic,
          controlled comparison of four distributed training strategies
          on an 8-GPU A100 server, with detailed metrics for GPU
          utilisation, memory, loss convergence, power consumption, and
          scaling efficiency (Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed}). The
          measurements show that ZeRO-3 delivers $22\%$ lower per-epoch
          energy than DP at the same final loss.
    \item \textbf{CUTLASS/CuTe mapping.} A mapping of the dense LLM
          computations (QKV, attention score/output, MLP, MoE) onto
          CUTLASS/CuTe GEMM pipelines, covering tile sizes, software
          pipelining, Tensor-Core utilisation, and epilogue fusion
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass}).
    \item \textbf{Sliding Window Flash Attention.} A novel SWFA algorithm
          with an efficient block-classification step and a vectorised
          mask generator that achieves up to $65.6\%$ reduction in
          computed blocks while preserving numerical accuracy within
          $10^{-4}$ tolerance (Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa}). A closed-form
          expression for the optimal window size under an exponential
          attention-decay model is also derived; strict convexity of the
          associated loss is proved.
    \item \textbf{SGLang kernel stack.} GPU kernel optimisations inside
          the SGLang runtime for long-context LLM serving, including
          paged KV cache, fused decode attention, SWFA prefill, and fused
          top-$k$/top-$p$ sampling. A layered evaluation shows a
          cumulative $2.84\times$ decode throughput speedup on LLaMA-7B
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}).
    \item \textbf{Triton profiling pipeline.} A profiling and auto-tuning
          pipeline for kernel-level roofline analysis, with shape-indexed
          configuration caching and a cross-validator that reports
          correctness and throughput against CUTLASS and Torch-inductor
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:triton}).
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility.} Algorithms, mask generators, and the
          block classifier are given as pseudocode in the main text and
          as reference PyTorch implementations in Appendix~A; full proofs
          of the convexity and optimal-window results, together with the
          block-count and communication-cost lemmas, are collected in
          Appendix~B.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope of the Work}\label{sec:intro_scope}
All experiments reported here are run on NVIDIA A100 GPUs housed in
an 8-GPU NVLink-connected server, with the CPU used as a
cross-checking reference wherever it is relevant---most importantly
during the SWFA numerical accuracy tests. For the distributed
training study, ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10 serves as a small, controlled
proxy, while a GPT-2 Medium-class model of about $355$M parameters
drives the end-to-end LLM throughput numbers. The SWFA kernel itself
is written in PyTorch, with CPU-optimised paths, and the code base
is structured so that a CUTLASS/Triton port can be slotted in later;
correctness is established on the reference implementation.

A number of topics are deliberately left outside the scope of this
thesis: (i) multi-node scaling, which is flagged as future work in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}; (ii) inference quantisation below
$8$~bits, which is largely orthogonal to the kernel-level
optimisations on which this thesis concentrates; and
(iii) alignment and RLHF workloads, whose bottlenecks are quite
different. For each of these, the relevant literature is pointed to
so that the results of this thesis can be located within the wider
picture.

\section{Methodology Overview}\label{sec:intro_methodology}
Every technical chapter from Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed} through
Chapter~\ref{ch:triton} obeys the same internal layout, so that any
one of them can be read on its own without losing sight of how it
plugs into the larger stack:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Background.} A concise restatement of the relevant
          concepts, calibrated to the specific layer of the stack.
    \item \textbf{Methodology.} An explicit \emph{Methodology} section
          describing the experimental setup, the workload, the metrics,
          and the hardware/software versions. The same hardware
          description is used across chapters so that numbers from
          different chapters can be compared directly.
    \item \textbf{Implementation.} Either pseudocode or reference
          PyTorch/Triton snippets that are sufficient for an independent
          reimplementation.
    \item \textbf{Results.} Quantitative results on the workload of the
          chapter, always accompanied by tables and figures whose source
          data is reproducible from the reference implementation.
    \item \textbf{Discussion.} A brief discussion of what the numbers
          mean and how they compose with the rest of the stack.
\end{enumerate}
Keeping the experimental setup adjacent to the measurements it
generates---rather than collapsing everything into one monolithic
methodology chapter spanning five unrelated systems---makes the
numbers easier to audit and easier to reproduce in isolation.

\section{Timeline}\label{sec:intro_timeline}
The work reported in this thesis was carried out in four phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 --- Distributed training (months 1--3).}
          Baseline DP/DDP/FSDP/ZeRO-3 implementations, setup of the
          8-GPU A100 server, NCCL/DeepSpeed tuning, and the controlled
          comparison of Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed}.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 --- Kernel layer (months 3--6).} CUTLASS/CuTe
          study of Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass}, including the LLM-layer
          mapping and epilogue fusion micro-benchmarks.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 --- Attention algorithm (months 6--9).}
          Design and implementation of SWFA
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa}), numerical validation against a
          vanilla reference, and the optimal-window derivation.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4 --- Serving and profiling (months 9--12).}
          SGLang kernel engineering
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}) and the Triton profiling pipeline
          (Chapter~\ref{ch:triton}), which together close the loop
          between algorithmic design and on-GPU performance.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The rest of the thesis proceeds in the following way.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:litreview} --- Literature Review.}
          Reviews the prior art across distributed training, GEMM
          libraries, efficient-attention mechanisms, LLM serving
          systems, and GPU kernel profiling, locates the present
          work against the gaps that survey reveals, and sets up
          the vocabulary used in subsequent chapters.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:distributed} --- Distributed
          Computing and Training.} Develops the methodology and then
          runs the controlled comparison among DP, DDP, FSDP, and
          DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 on the 8-GPU A100 node. The chapter
          identifies which strategy yields the highest GPU
          utilisation and the lowest energy per epoch on a common
          workload, and fixes the per-layer memory budget that the
          remaining layers of the stack must live within.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass} --- CUTLASS and CuTe GEMM
          Operations.} Covers GEMM kernels built on top of CUTLASS
          together with the CuTe layout algebra, and then maps the
          dense operations in an LLM---QKV, attention score and
          output, MLP, and grouped MoE---onto the Tensor-Core
          hierarchy. The epilogue fusions developed here reappear
          later as the writeback path of the attention-output GEMM
          in Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} and of the logit-projection GEMM
          in Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa} --- Sliding Window Flash
          Attention.} Presents SWFA: the block classifier, the
          vectorised mask generator, the forward and backward
          passes, and a closed-form expression for the optimal
          window size accompanied by a proof that the associated
          loss is strictly convex. SWFA consumes the GEMM primitives
          of Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass} and is slotted, later in the
          thesis, into the prefill kernel of
          Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang} --- GPU Kernels in the
          SGLang Runtime.} Addresses the serving-side of the stack:
          the paged KV cache, the fused decode attention, the SWFA
          prefill kernel, and fused top-$k$/top-$p$ sampling. A
          layered ablation then decomposes the cumulative decode
          speedup into the share contributed by each individual
          optimisation.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:triton} --- Triton Kernel Profiling
          and Auto-tuning.} Introduces the Triton-based profiling
          and auto-tuning pipeline that serves as an independent
          cross-check on the CUTLASS numbers of
          Chapter~\ref{ch:cutlass}, the SWFA numbers of
          Chapter~\ref{ch:swfa}, and the serving numbers of
          Chapter~\ref{ch:sglang}. The shape-indexed configuration
          cache introduced here is the mechanism that keeps the
          kernel-level decisions valid as shapes and hardware
          generations evolve.
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} --- Conclusions and
          Future Work.} Recaps the contributions, extracts the
          cross-chapter lessons, and lays out near-, medium-, and
          long-term research directions.
\end{itemize}

Algorithmic pseudocode, the accompanying reference PyTorch listings,
and the hardware/software details needed to reproduce the
experiments are gathered in Appendix~A. The full proofs of the
theoretical claims made in the main text are kept together in
Appendix~B. Every theorem in the main text carries a link to its
proof, and every algorithm carries a link to its reference code, so
that any statement in the thesis can be traced back to its
supporting evidence in at most two clicks.
